Attachment Styles
Attachment theory, rooted in the work of John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, identifies four primary styles based on early caregiving experiences. Questions should assess comfort with intimacy, independence, and emotional regulation in relationships.
Sample Questions:
Secure Attachment
"Do you feel comfortable relying on others and having them rely on you?"
"How easy is it for you to trust a partner in a relationship?"
Scale: 1 (Not at all) to 5 (Very much)
Anxious Attachment
"Do you often worry that your partner might leave you or doesn’t care enough?"
"How frequently do you need reassurance from a partner about their feelings?"
Scale: 1 (Rarely) to 5 (Constantly)
Avoidant Attachment
"Do you prefer to keep your independence rather than get too close to someone?"
"How do you feel when a partner wants to discuss deep emotions with you?"
Scale: 1 (Very comfortable) to 5 (Very uncomfortable)
Fearful-Avoidant Attachment
"Do you ever feel torn between wanting closeness and being afraid of it?"
"How often do you push people away even though you want them to stay?"
Scale: 1 (Never) to 5 (Frequently)
Insights for Users:
"You show traits of a secure attachment style, meaning you’re likely comfortable with both closeness and independence. This flexibility can make you compatible with most partners."
"Your responses suggest an anxious attachment style, where you might benefit from a partner who offers consistent reassurance."
Research Basis:
Hazan and Shaver (1987) developed early self-report measures for adult attachment.
Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991) refined the four-category model (secure, anxious, avoidant, fearful-avoidant).
Conflict Resolution Patterns
Drawing from John Gottman’s research, particularly his identification of the "Four Horsemen" and repair attempts, questions should evaluate how individuals approach disagreements and recover from them.
Sample Questions:
Constructive Communication
"When you disagree with someone, how likely are you to express your feelings calmly and directly?"
Scale: 1 (Not likely) to 5 (Very likely)
Validation
"Do you try to understand your partner’s point of view, even if you don’t agree?"
Scale: 1 (Rarely) to 5 (Always)
Compromise Willingness
"How willing are you to meet a partner halfway during an argument?"
Scale: 1 (Not willing) to 5 (Very willing)
Repair Attempts
"After a fight, how often do you try to lighten the mood or apologize to reconnect?"
Scale: 1 (Never) to 5 (Often)
Four Horsemen
Criticism: "Do you tend to blame your partner’s character (e.g., 'You’re so selfish') rather than the situation?"
Contempt: "How often do you feel superior or sarcastic toward a partner during conflict?"
Defensiveness: "When criticized, do you usually make excuses or counterattack?"
Stonewalling: "Do you shut down or withdraw during arguments?"
Scale for each: 1 (Never) to 5 (Frequently)
Insights for Users:
"You lean toward constructive communication and validation, which are strong predictors of healthy conflict resolution."
"Your tendency toward defensiveness or stonewalling might create challenges unless paired with a patient, communicative partner."
Research Basis:
Gottman’s longitudinal studies (e.g., Gottman & Levenson, 1992) show the Four Horsemen predict divorce with over 90% accuracy.
Repair attempts are critical for relationship resilience (Gottman, 1999).
Shared Values
Alignment on core values stabilizes relationships. Questions should assess priorities and preferences across key domains, weighted by their importance to the individual.
Sample Questions:
Life Goals
"How important is career success to you?" (1-5)
"Would you relocate for a partner’s ambitions?" (Yes/No/Maybe)
Financial Attitudes
"Are you more of a saver or a spender?” (Saver / Balanced / Spender)
"How important is financial security to you?" (1-5)
Family Planning
"Do you want children?" (Yes / No / Unsure)
"If yes, how many and how soon?" (Open response)
Religious/Spiritual Beliefs
"How important is religion or spirituality in your life?" (1-5)
"Would you need a partner to share your beliefs?" (Yes/No/Maybe)
Lifestyle Preferences
"Do you prefer a structured routine or spontaneity?" (Structured / Flexible / Mix)
"How much do you value socializing vs. alone time?" (1-5)
Political/Social Views
"How important are your political beliefs to your identity?" (1-5)
"Could you date someone with opposing views on major issues?" (Yes/No/Maybe)
Insights for Users:
"You prioritize financial security and a structured lifestyle, so a partner with similar values might suit you best."
"Your openness to differing political views suggests flexibility in relationships."
Research Basis:
Studies like those by Luo and Klohnen (2005) show value similarity predicts relationship satisfaction more than personality similarity.
The American Psychological Association highlights shared goals as a cornerstone of long-term compatibility.
Implementation Approach
Scientifically Validated Questions
Use established tools like the Experiences in Close Relationships Scale (ECR) for attachment, Gottman’s conflict inventories, and value-alignment surveys.
Ensure questions are concise, reliable, and predictive of relationship outcomes.
Weighting in Matching Algorithm
Attachment compatibility: 35% (secure matches broadly, avoidant-anxious pairs flagged as risky).
Conflict resolution: 35% (penalize Four Horsemen traits, prioritize repair attempts).
Shared values: 30% (weight higher for issues rated as "very important" by users).
User Insights
Provide a summary: "Based on your answers, you’re securely attached, handle conflict constructively, and value family planning highly. You’d likely thrive with someone who mirrors these traits."
Matching Strategy
Prioritize complementary styles (e.g., secure with any, anxious with secure) over exact similarity.
Flag mismatches (e.g., high contempt with low validation) with explanations.
